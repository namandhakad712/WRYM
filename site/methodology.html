Deep Work · The Analysis

Read the rulebooks before building the arena

WRYM is not a novel idea in a vacuum — it stands on a decade of game-based agent evaluation. Before building the v2 rating stack we went back to the primary sources: the papers, the leaderboards, the harnesses. This page is the honest ledger of that analysis: for each system, what it does best, and which specific pattern we lifted and shipped. Every claim is cited; every citation was opened and verified on 6 November 2026. The full conformance table is at the bottom; the philosophy lives in the paper.

Kaggle Game Arena

Google DeepMind × Kaggle · 2025

An open platform where models compete head-to-head in strategic games — launched August 2025 with chess, then expanded (Feb 2026) to poker and Werewolf to test social dynamics and calculated risk. Ranking is pooled Bradley-Terry Elo across games and models.

Sharpest idea: games resist saturation. There is no answer key to leak — a new map is a new test — and adversarial play makes the benchmark evergreen.
Adopted: the pooled Bradley-Terry estimator with bootstrap confidence intervals (our src/lib/wyrm/elo.ts v2), the head-to-head matrix on the leaderboard, and the evergreen doctrine — the Level Forge + procedural generators keep producing fresh maps forever.
Kaggle (2025), Introducing Kaggle Game Arena · Google (2026), Advancing AI benchmarking with Game Arena

Chatbot Arena / LMArena

LMSYS · ICML 2024

The platform that made pairwise comparison the industry standard: anonymous side-by-side battles, human votes, and Elo ratings — later refined to Bradley-Terry MLE with bootstrapped confidence intervals over the whole battle dataset at once.

Sharpest idea: order-free pooled ratings + honest uncertainty. "Is A better than B?" is only answerable when the CI is on the table. Their 2023-12 Elo update post is the reference write-up.
Adopted: exactly that estimator — BT-MLE over all shared-level battles, 400-round deterministic bootstrap for 95% CIs, draws counted as half-wins — replacing our old order-dependent online Elo (K=32). Every rating on the leaderboard now renders as Elo ±CI.
Chiang et al. (2024), arXiv:2403.04132 · LMSYS (2023), New models & Elo system update

BALROG

ICLR 2025 · arXiv:2411.13543

Six RL game environments (BabyAI, Crafter, TextWorld, Baba Is AI, MiniHack, NetHack) as one long-horizon agentic benchmark. Frontier models partially succeed on easy games and collapse on NetHack; VLMs do worse with pixels than with text.

Sharpest ideas: ① fine-grained progress metrics — how close to completion, not just win/loss; ② the knowing-doing gap (models verbalize correct knowledge, then fail to act on it); ③ procedural generation to defeat memorization.
Adopted: the progress score — every archived run now carries a 0-100 partial-credit value (60% apples, 25% goal, 15% distance closure) computed by src/lib/wyrm/progress.ts; and our failure-seeking difficulty ladder is their "the information is in the failure" doctrine taken literally. Our text-first interface is also their empirical finding (text > pixels) turned into a design rule.
Paglieri et al. (2024), arXiv:2411.13543 · balrogai.com

LMGame-Bench

ICLR 2026 · arXiv:2505.15146

"How good are LLMs at playing games?" — real, contamination-robust games (Wordle, 2048, Sudoku, Sokoban…) turned into controlled benchmarks, evaluating models with and without a gaming harness (scaffolding that parses state and validates moves).

Sharpest ideas: ① measure the harness delta — the same model raw vs scaffolded; ② contamination control through game variation rather than held-out answers.
Adopted: the contamination answer — every ladder level now has a certified mirror twin (horizontal flip, valves remapped, gravity preserved for eggs, identical par; see results/variants.json, 38/38 certified). A model that memorized the original but cannot reason will fail its twin. Our invalid-action feedback loop (rejected moves return a machine reason) is their harness finding operationalized.
Hu et al. (2025), arXiv:2505.15146

SmartPlay

arXiv:2310.01557 · 2023

Six games (Rock-Paper-Scissors → Tower of Hanoi → Minecraft) decomposing agent skill into nine named capabilities — object dependencies, planning ahead, spatial reasoning, learning from history, randomness — with each game stressing a known subset, so results decompose into a capability profile instead of one scalar.

Sharpest idea: the benchmark as a diagnostic roadmap: "which capability is missing?" beats "what's the score?".
Adopted: the full pattern — our seven-capability taxonomy (src/lib/wyrm/capabilities.ts: spatial, planning, dependencies, state-tracking, long-horizon, risk, precision) derived deterministically from each level's geometry, and per-model capability profiles on the leaderboard, weighted by intensity.
Wu, Tang, Mitchell & Li (2023), arXiv:2310.01557

VideoGameBench

arXiv:2505.18134 · 2025

Ten famous 1990s video games played by VLMs from raw pixels in real time. The finding: frontier VLMs often cannot reach even the first checkpoint of games humans learn in minutes.

Sharpest idea: the modality axis is a first-class ablation — the same game, same model, different observation channel (text vs vision), is a controlled experiment.
Adopted: the dual-view doctrine, deliberately inverted — models receive the deterministic text projection while humans watch the pixel-perfect canvas render of the same state. Our run schema records the modality axis explicitly so a vision channel (canvas → PNG → VLM) can be benchmarked later without schema surgery. BALROG's text>pixels result is why we start text-first.
Zhang et al. (2025), arXiv:2505.18134

Terminal-Bench

Laude Institute × Stanford · 2026

Hard, realistic terminal tasks, each with its own environment, human-written solution and test suite. The public leaderboard shows resolution rate ± CI per model × agent harness, with token and cost columns — and an error analysis to close the loop.

Sharpest ideas: ① the model × harness matrix; ② publish cost and tokens next to every score; ③ structured failure analysis, not vibes.
Adopted: the failure taxonomy — every model row now decomposes failures into spike deaths, turn-budget exhaustion, invalid-action storms and API aborts, computed from per-step event logs by the exporter; plus tokens/latency/cost columns on every row (cost accounting was already in our reliability ledger; now it ranks).
Merrill, Shaw, Carlini et al. (2026), arXiv:2601.11868 · tbench.ai

AgentBench

arXiv:2308.03688 · 2023

The systematic "LLM-as-Agent" benchmark: eight distinct environments (OS, DB, knowledge graphs, a digital card game, lateral-thinking puzzles, ALFWorld households, web shopping…) unified behind one harness protocol.

Sharpest idea: one harness, many environments — evaluation as infrastructure, not as a pile of scripts. Environments are interchangeable behind a common step interface.

Adopted: the doctrine that the game is a pluggable environment behind a fixed protocol — our engine, checker, sandbox and rating layers never know which level (or level variant) they are running; new environments slot in as more LevelDef rows or community PRs without touching the pipeline.
Liu et al. (2023), arXiv:2308.03688 · github.com/THUDM/AgentBench

Deliberate divergences — where we consciously differ
  • Adversarial play is against the board, not another model. Kaggle's games are model-vs-model; our worm is model-vs-certified-geometry, so the reference line (WYRM-BFS, provably optimal) can anchor every rating — no judge, no opponent luck, no collusion surface.
  • Text-first on purpose. Both BALROG and VideoGameBench measured vision degrading play; we ship the deterministic text projection as the primary channel and keep pixels for humans.
  • Failure-seeking difficulty. Most benchmarks tune for discrimination at the top; our Tier IV-V is tuned past the frontier so failure modes are visible — the information is in the failure.
