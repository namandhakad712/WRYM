WRYM: The Worm Arena

A grid-worm puzzle benchmark where language models play a PSPACE-flavoured game under a text-only projection — and every claim is certified by an exhaustive solver.

⤓ full PDF repository design research dossier MCP model plug-in guide
TL;DR

We benchmark language models on WOIM-style grid-worm puzzles — deterministic, turn-based, no luck, no dexterity. Models see the board as text only and must plan: eat every apple, manage body length, push crates, press plates, respect one-way valves, burn bridges, dig dirt, carry keys — and cut themselves in half when flesh is the only weight that holds a door open. An exhaustive BFS solver certifies every level's solvability and par; a deterministic checker re-plays and signs every run. A seven-dimension difficulty index (depth, search width, irreversibility, simulation, threat, choreography, budget) measures difficulty from evidence on the optimal line — it refuses to inflate. The ladder tops out at The Rubicon, certified 9.0 / NIGHTMARE, designed from primary research on Snakebird, Stephen's Sausage Roll, Sokoban's PSPACE-completeness proofs and WOIM itself. Any model can plug in via MCP; any human can contribute levels via the browser Forge and a pull request. Everything — runs, replays, thoughts, certificates — is a static, reproducible archive.

1 · Why a worm game

Grid puzzle games are a uniquely clean instrument for measuring planning in language models: the state is fully observable and compactly serialisable, the rules are deterministic (no dice, no timers), and the difficulty is structural rather than perceptual. The worm genre adds the ingredient most benchmarks lack: the agent's own body is part of the state. Growth (eating apples) and self-amputation (splitting) turn the worm into both the mover and the tool — the mechanic WOIM (Dogspawn, 2026) made central, and which complexity theory takes seriously: item-collecting Snake is PSPACE-complete (De Biasi, 2018), as is Sokoban (Culberson, 1997).

2 · The arena contract

Every level is a rectangular grid over a 19-character vocabulary (walls, tunnels, apples, crates, plates, gates, spikes, dirt, keys, locks, four one-way valves, crumble bridges, ice, worm head/body, heart goal). Models receive a text projection of the board plus the full rule book, emit one action per turn (up / down / left / right / split), and are scored on solved levels, moves versus par, deaths, and waste. Invalid moves are rejected with reasons — free information, as in the real game. An exhaustive BFS validator certifies each level before it enters the ladder: solvable, optimal line, state count. A deterministic checker re-executes every recorded run; mismatches void the run.

3 · The difficulty index (v3)

Difficulty is measured, not asserted. The index replays the certified optimal solution through the real engine and records: plan length (depth), BFS states explored (search, log scale), permanent world mutations (irreversibility), forced ice glides (simulation), the fraction of legal alternatives that end the run (threat), sub-goal ordering load (choreography), and turn-limit pressure (budget). Each dimension saturates smoothly to 100; the composite blends the weighted mean with the strongest dimension and maps to tiers TUTORIAL → NIGHTMARE. A level containing no ice can never score on simulation; no spikes, never on threat. In the whole 31-level ladder exactly one level reaches NIGHTMARE.

4 · Tier V — the extreme-level research

The five levels of the Fifth Circle were designed from primary sources: Snakebird's "single wrong button press" unwinnability and fruit-order length contracts; Stephen's Sausage Roll's chains of irreversible acts; the corridor-register gadgets of the Sokoban PSPACE proof; nuworm's action-at-a-distance; and WOIM's cut-yourself-in-half economy. The certified optimal lines exhibit behaviours we did not explicitly design — in Absolute Zero and The Rubicon the worm splits mid-route so that its own inert remains become the slide-stoppers the next ice glide requires. 5 · Models, MCP, and the fully-static archive

Any model can enter: the arena speaks OpenAI-style chat over pluggable adapters, and an MCP server (mcp/llm-bridge.mjs) exposes the game as tools so MCP-capable clients can play without custom code. Runs execute locally (the repository is the lab); their JSON artifacts — every step, every thought, reliability telemetry, checker signature — are committed, and GitHub Pages renders them with zero backend: leaderboard, level atlas, certified replays with the model's mind alongside the board. Reproducibility is the user interface.
